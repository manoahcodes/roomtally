Tiling is a job where a small mistake is expensive. Too few tiles and you may not match the batch later. Too many and you pay for tiles that sit in the garage. This guide shows how layout, grout and waste decide the number you buy.

Every figure here is an estimate. Check the coverage on your own box, and ask your supplier what they advise for your layout.

## The short version

Work out how many tiles cover the surface, add waste for cuts, then round up to whole boxes.

**Tiles = surface area ÷ [(tile width + gap) × (tile height + gap)] × (1 + waste ÷ 100).**

In words: divide the surface area by the area one tile takes up with its gap. Then add the waste.

## Step 1: Measure the surface

Measure the floor or the wall you will tile. For a floor, multiply length by width. For a wall, multiply the width by the height.

For walls, take off doors and windows. The guide on [how to measure a room](/guides/how-to-measure-a-room/) covers this.

## Step 2: Account for the grout gap

Each tile sits in a small space of grout. The gap is usually 2 to 5 mm, or about 1/8 inch. Beautally starts with 3 mm, or 1/8 inch, and lets you change it.

The tile plus its gap is the area each tile really uses. For a 300 mm tile with a 3 mm gap, that is 303 mm on each side. The effect is small on one tile and noticeable across a whole floor.

## Step 3: Choose your layout

The pattern you lay changes how many tiles are cut. More cuts mean more waste.

| Layout | Typical waste | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Straight | 10% | Few cuts, mostly at the edges. |
| Diagonal | 15% | Every edge tile is cut at an angle. |
| Herringbone | 20% | Many angled cuts and short offcuts. |

These are typical trade allowances, not rules. A small room with many corners needs more. Large tiles also waste more, because each cut throws away more of the tile.

## Step 4: Add the waste and round up

Multiply the tile count by one plus the waste. Round up to whole tiles. Then divide the tile area by the area one box covers and round up to whole boxes.

You order boxes, so the box count is the number that counts. Beautally shows it first as the buy quantity, with the unrounded need beside it.

## Worked example: a 4 m by 3.5 m floor

The floor is 14 m². The tiles are 300 mm by 300 mm with a 3 mm gap. Each box covers 1 m².

One tile and its gap take up 0.303 × 0.303 = 0.0918 m². So 14 m² holds about 152.5 tiles before waste.

| Layout | Waste | Tiles with waste | Tile area | Boxes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Straight | 10% | 168 | 15.10 m² | 16 |
| Diagonal | 15% | 176 | 15.78 m² | 16 |
| Herringbone | 20% | 183 | 16.47 m² | 17 |

The tile area is the number of tiles, with waste, times the face area of one tile (0.09 m²).

For the straight lay you need 15.10 m², so 16 boxes. The diagonal lay needs 15.78 m², which still fits in 16 boxes. Herringbone needs 16.47 m², which is more than 16 boxes cover, so it needs 17.

## How the gap changes the count

On the same 14 m² floor with a straight lay and 300 mm tiles:

- A 2 mm gap needs 169 tiles.
- A 3 mm gap needs 168 tiles.
- A 10 mm gap needs 161 tiles.

A wider gap uses more grout and fewer tiles. It also changes the look of the floor, so choose the gap for the design first.

## Large tiles

Bigger tiles cover more per piece, so you buy fewer of them. The same floor in 600 mm tiles, straight lay, needs about 43 tiles. That is 15.25 m², or 16 boxes.

The box count barely moves. The risk grows, though. One miscut large tile wastes more than one small tile. If the room is small or the cuts are tricky, add more waste.

## Keep a spare box

Tiles from different batches can differ in shade and size. A box bought later may not match. Buy everything from the same batch, and keep a spare box or a few loose tiles for repairs.

If you want a repair reserve, add a box to your order or raise the waste in the options.

## Tiling more than one room

If several rooms use the same tile, add their areas first. Apply the waste once, then round up once. Rounding each room on its own leaves part-used boxes.

Beautally adds rooms together first and rounds up once to whole boxes.

## Common mistakes

- Leaving out the waste.
- Using a straight-lay allowance for a diagonal pattern.
- Forgetting the grout gap when you count tiles.
- Rounding down. Always round up to whole boxes.
- Mixing batches, so the shade changes across the floor.
